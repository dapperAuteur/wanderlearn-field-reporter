## Block 3 — Word-for-word VO (verbatim)

**[Beat 1 · talking-head · 0:00]**
> The very first lesson made one promise: reflection is a graph, not a prompt. Everything since has been
> earning that sentence. Stand at the end now and look back down the staircase.

**[Beat 2 · slide: six beliefs · 0:25]**
> Each module installed one belief. Module 0: reflection is a graph, not a prompt. Module 1: the cap lives in
> code, counting is the graph's job. Module 2: writing checks an LLM can score is a craft. Module 3: tracing
> makes bugs legible, not rarer. Module 4: the runtime rubric is also the offline test. Module 5: reflection
> is a budget allocation, not a magic wand. And the capstone: you can redeploy the whole pattern, without the
> notes, on a domain you have never seen.

**[Beat 3 · slide: one method · 1:30]**
> Read that column top to bottom and it is not seven tricks, it is one method. A reflection loop is a cyclic
> graph, with a guaranteed exit, steered by a data rubric an LLM can score, made legible by tracing, protected
> by an eval that reuses the rubric, and allocated as a budget on a cost-quality curve. And because every
> piece is generic, the whole thing transfers.

**[Beat 4 · talking-head · 2:20]**
> Here is the anti-pattern the whole course refutes: reflection is magic. Treating a loop as a sophisticated
> thing you add on faith. The course's entire arc refutes it. Reflection is a bounded, judged, traced,
> evaluated, priced graph, and sometimes the right amount of it is none. You do not believe in reflection
> loops now. You measure them.

**[Beat 5 · talking-head · 3:05]**
> Go back and reread Lesson 1's last line: every hard question in this course is tractable precisely because
> the loop is a graph you can inspect. You have now inspected it node by node, bounded it, judged it, traced
> it, evaluated it, priced it, and carried it to new ground. The mental model is installed. The notes were
> scaffolding. Take them down. Pick a domain we never named, write its rubric, build the loop from the parts
> in examples, run the eval, read the dashboard, and ship it. You can. That's the course. Thanks for building
> it with me.
